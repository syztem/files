The draft is the score. The cloth is the performance&

Every patterned weave begins as a grid of decisions made long before a single thread is wet. Three of them — threading, treadling, tie‑up — and everything after is arithmetic. Below, six structures are drawn, read and woven by the browser itself. Nothing here is an image of cloth. It is cloth.

Plate II  ·  The Score threading × tie‑up × treadling

Three squares, one truth

A draft is the whole of a weave, written down. The threading says which shaft each warp end is drawn through. The tie‑up says which shafts each treadle lifts. The treadling says the order the treadles are pressed. Read the first two as a little machine, and the third as the melody you play on it. Hold the pointer over any square to trace the thread through the machine.

Plate III  ·  The Dyes colour‑and‑weave

Structure gives the figure.
Dye gives the ground.

Colour in a patterned weave is almost never decoration applied afterwards. It is assigned thread by thread before weaving, then covered or exposed by the draft itself. The palette below is drawn from the natural dyes a small mill would actually keep: indigo and madder, weld and oak gall, woad and walnut, and the undyed fleece that, in old cloth, was simply whatever the animal wore.

Plate IV  ·  Notes on reading a draft

How to read a square

The first thing to understand about a weaving draft is that it is not a picture of anything. It is a program — a short list of instructions that, read one end and one pick at a time, produces an arbitrary distance away. This is the same situation as sheet music, and the analogy is older than you might guess: the first European treatises on figured weaving were copied from the same scribal hands that copied the chant.

At every crossing of warp and weft, exactly one thread must lie on top. That single binary decision, repeated across the whole cloth, is the whole of weaving. Everything else — the sheen, the drape, the name of the pattern — is downstream of it.

The three parts

Threading runs across the top. Read each column downward: the filled square tells you which shaft that end of the warp is heddled on. Tie‑up is the small matrix at the corner, the loom's wiring: for each treadle, which shafts are lifted. Treadling runs down the side and is simply the order of the treadles. Multiply the three and you get the drawdown, which is the cloth, flat.

warp_up(i, j) = tie[ treadling[ j ] ].includes( threading[ i ] )

That single line of code is the whole of the engine behind every cloth on this page. It has no randomness in it and no image files. Change the threading and you have a different weave; change the colour assigned to each end and you have the same weave in a different cloth.

A small fact worth knowing On four shafts, plain weave and a 2/2 twill are the same cloth. The twill line simply steps across the shaft order and, by the time it returns, the floats have already closed. Weavers have known this for centuries; it is why sett is counted in shafts and why the smallest useful number of them is four.

Point, and the diamond

If the threading runs up the shafts and then back down again, the interlacement reverses with it, and a twill line folds on itself. The result is a point draft: the surface picks, and a set of lozenges opens along the fold. On eight shafts the diamonds are wide enough to read as architecture. This is the structure on Plate I, and it is the reason a point draft looks so much more like a design than a straight twill does.

Overshot, the last of the six, works by a different trick. Four shafts weave a plain or twilled ground; a fifth carries a separate pattern warp that never interlaces at all. It simply rides over the top of the ground on certain picks and disappears beneath it on others. Because the pattern warp is never cut by the weft, the shapes it makes have hard, clean edges in a way no ground weave can manage.

Group those riding picks one, then two, then four, then four, then two, then one, and you get the pointed oval at the heart of a rosepath. It is a shape you can get by no other means, and it is the oldest pattern in this collection by a very long way.

What the colour is doing

In colour‑and‑weave, a pattern is made by giving each thread its own colour and then weaving the structure over the top of that arrangement. Two colourways and a plain weave will do it. What surprises people is how much the same two colours will do with a different draft: the cloth changes character entirely, not merely in its detail but in its apparent grain.

On balance Every weave that hangs properly is balanced: as many warp threads on top as weft. Try the balance readout in the left rail. The plain and herringbone cloths sit at zero. The warp‑faced twill runs heavily positive, the rosepath negative — both of which are exactly why those structures drape and hang the way they do.

Why this page exists

Because it is a small, complete argument for how much is contained in a grid. Six structures, six colourways, twenty‑four dyed threads of repeat, and no pixels of cloth anywhere — only the score, and a browser willing to perform it. The loom was always just a way of reading a square.

Change anything. The draft is the score, and a score exists to be played by somebody else.

Method

Every crossing of the cloth is resolved from tie[treadling[j]].includes(threading[i]). Threads are merged into maximal float runs and stroked as SVG paths, so a 40 × 40 sample costs about a hundred path segments rather than two thousand rectangles.

Provenance

Structures after the standard hand‑loom repertoire: plain weave, warp‑faced twill, five‑end satin, herringbone, point twill and five‑shaft overshot. Woven and computed in a browser. Set in the reader's own serif.
